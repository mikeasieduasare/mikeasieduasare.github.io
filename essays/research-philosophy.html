Research Philosophy

How I think about AI as a technical and social system, and what it means to be responsible for it.

Michael Asiedu Asare September 2026 4 min read

A model can be right in the laboratory and wrong in a clinic in Kumasi without a single line of its code changing. Nothing inside it breaks. The world around it moves: the patients are different, the scanner is newer, the questions arrive in another language. The model does not notice. It keeps answering with the same confidence it had on the day it was tested. This is why I think of AI systems as engineered systems, and as social systems too. The question I care about is not only whether a system works. It is what makes it work, how those properties change, whether we can notice them degrading before a failure has real consequences, and who bears those consequences when we do not. Once a system leaves the lab, it becomes part of hospitals, schools, markets, and governments. From then on, its reliability belongs as much to the people and institutions that depend on it as to the technology itself.

On the technical side, I am especially interested in the structure beneath a system’s visible behavior. A learned system has representations, parameters, computational pathways, objectives, and dependencies that its outputs do not always reveal. When these change, what the system knows can change with them, along with how it handles uncertainty and how reliably it behaves. The same is true of its environment. Distribution shift, adversarial interference, limited resources, and compression can all leave a system giving plausible answers after the basis for those answers has worn away. Part of how I approach AI safety is as the study of how structural change turns into behavioral change.

I believe reliability has to be studied as something that moves. A system does not always go straight from working to failing. Along the way, uncertainty may rise, representations may shift, and calibration may slip while the usual performance measures still look fine. These in-between states matter because they may leave room to intervene. I am not content to record that a model failed after the fact. I want to know whether the path toward failure has a structure we can detect, whether that structure can be described precisely, and whether it can be used to make systems safer before they fail.

The same reasoning applies when we change a system’s computational structure. Models are rarely deployed exactly as they were trained. They are quantized, pruned, distilled, or squeezed onto limited hardware, and these changes are usually judged by accuracy, speed, and cost. Yet a system can keep its accuracy and still lose the properties that reliable deployment depends on. This has a social edge. The cheaper, compressed version of a model is often the one that reaches clinics and communities with the fewest resources. If we only check that accuracy survives, the least examined version of a system may end up serving the people with the fewest alternatives.

This is where engineering meets questions that engineering alone cannot settle. To engineer something is to keep its behavior within acceptable bounds despite uncertainty. But deciding what counts as acceptable is a social and moral choice. Someone decides which failures are tolerable, whose languages and data a system is tested on, and who absorbs the risk when it goes wrong. These decisions are political whether or not we call them that. A medical assistant evaluated only in English has quietly been made safe for some of its users and not others. A monitoring threshold encodes a judgment about how much harm we will accept before anyone steps in. I want those judgments made in the open, by people who can be held to account, rather than left buried in default settings.

Thinking this way connects fields that are usually kept apart. Robustness asks how systems behave when conditions change. Verification asks what we can guarantee about their behavior. Security asks how someone could undermine them on purpose. Interpretability asks what we can learn about the mechanisms behind their behavior. Alignment and controllability ask whether more capable systems keep responding to the goals and corrections we set. Safe deployment asks what happens when they meet a world more complicated than the one they were built in. Governance asks who gets to decide how they are built, tested, and used, and on whose behalf. To me, these are different angles on one problem: building intelligent systems whose important properties we can understand, measure, and control, within institutions able to act on what we learn.

In the end, I see this work as stewardship. Those of us who build consequential technologies are responsible, to a large degree, for what they do in the world, including effects that appear slowly and far from where we work. Safety should not be a layer added once a system is already capable. It should grow with the system, through better measurement, stronger theory, and careful evaluation, and it should answer to the people the system affects. My aim is to contribute to that foundation while keeping a larger question in view: whether the systems we build leave people healthier, freer, and more able to shape their own lives. The technologies worth building are the ones that keep earning our trust as conditions change, and that leave the world better for the people who inherit it.
